# S17 · Platform, performance & reliability

Products rated: KerfDesk, LightBurn 2.1.03 (Core $99 / Pro $199), MillMage 0.8.02 (CNC-only — S17 is platform/performance/reliability, not a laser-only sector, so its desktop platform and stability are rated here), LaserGRBL 7.14.1 (dormant ~16 months), XCS (= xTool Studio desktop V1.7.x per phase-0 roster note), Easel (web SaaS, Free/Starter/Pro), gSender (Sienci Labs, free — the roster's sender reference).
N/R: none — every roster product ships as installable or web software and therefore competes on platform coverage, performance and reliability. No cell is `U`: documentary or code evidence was found for all 42.
Pinned evidence: KerfDesk @ e0a10b6c (worktree HEAD at rating time; evidence pack `data/S17-evidence.json` — entirely static code/config reading plus prior-audit reuse, **no runtime profiling, no dev server, no hardware, no benchmarking of any kind this pass**); competitor evidence `data/S17-market.json`, versions per `data/phase0.json`.

### State of play

S17 is the sector where KerfDesk's engineering *intent* is genuinely careful but its *evidence* is at its thinnest, and the scorecard reflects both facts: KerfDesk leads no category here, sits mid-pack in most, and is the roster's weakest on resource footprint. Its sector mean (~5.8) is honest and matches the persona weights that put S17 in the middle of the deck. The structural reason is stated up front in the pinned-evidence line — nothing in this sector was measured. Every performance number (17.3), every footprint number (17.6), and the physical half of crash recovery (17.4) is `[CODE]` static call-path analysis, not a profiler run, a memory snapshot, or a real force-kill. Per the audit's no-benefit-of-the-doubt rule, thoughtful caps that were never observed to hold cannot score as if they were proven, so KerfDesk's careful budgets earn a "solid-by-design" 5, not a "fast" 8.

Platform coverage (17.1) is the sector's clearest structural story, and it favours the web-native products. Easel leads at 9 with the broadest reach in the roster — Windows, macOS, Linux and Chromebook through any of several browsers, tablet included — with its only real cost (a hard internet dependency) charged in 17.2, not here. KerfDesk, XCS and gSender form a three-way 7-cluster from three different shapes of "broad but not widest": KerfDesk reaches every desktop OS through its Chromium PWA and adds a native Windows `.exe`, but has no tablet/mobile UI and the packaged `.exe` was never actually built or run; XCS covers two desktops plus real iOS/Android/iPad apps but no Linux and no web; gSender covers all three desktops natively plus Raspberry Pi but has no web or mobile. LightBurn (6) and MillMage (5) are the dual-desktop pair — LightBurn keeps a frozen legacy-1.7 Linux build, MillMage dropped Linux entirely — and LaserGRBL (3) is Windows-only, the roster's narrowest.

Offline and install/update (17.2) is where KerfDesk's design is most differentiated but not category-leading. Its fully-offline installable PWA plus a genuinely thoughtful update model — the update banner is suppressed mid-burn and "Later" persists per app version, killing the repeat-nag bug — is real, code-verified work, and it ties LightBurn and MillMage at 7. But gSender wins the category at 9 with the roster's smoothest all-around story: a free installer on all four platforms, fully offline, an in-app update guide, and frequent releases. Two things keep KerfDesk off gSender's pace and honest at 7: the desktop auto-update ships **unsigned with no code-signature verification** (a security-adjacent tamper window, confirmed from config), and none of the offline-install or update behavior was ever exercised on a device. The cloud-tethered products (XCS, Easel) and dormant LaserGRBL trail at 5–6.

Stability and crash recovery (17.4) shows KerfDesk's design at its richest and its proof at its most caveated. On paper it is comprehensive — a network-free ErrorBoundary crash screen, window-level global error/rejection handlers, a 30-second localStorage autosave with a boot restore prompt, and a fingerprint-verified job checkpoint that recompiles-and-compares to resume — and corrupt slots are discarded so startup cannot brick. That is arguably a deeper local design than LightBurn's autosave-plus-prompt (7). But it lands at 6, behind Easel's category-leading 8, for three honest reasons: Easel's continuous *server-side* save recovers a crashed tab with near-zero loss, KerfDesk carries two confirmed seam bugs (a phantom "Restore?" prompt because the restored slot is never cleared, and a selective/placed-job resume dead-end that reports the wrong "project was edited"), and the crash-plus-resume itself is HW-CLAIMED, never driven through a real force-kill. LaserGRBL (3, no recovery at all) and XCS (5, frequent import crashes) sit below.

Project-format robustness (17.5) splits cleanly into the half KerfDesk does very well and the half it barely does. Save *stability* is a genuine strength: the `.lf2` load path is a strictly validated, Result-typed trust boundary with real DoS caps (1e6 mm coordinate cap, 256M-px raster cap, luma cross-field checks) and a **dedicated security test corpus** — the only `[TEST]`-backed robustness evidence in this sector, and something no competitor evidence claims. But *versioning/migration* is immature: the schema is frozen at v1 with an empty migrator registry, so an older build silently drops unknown blocks on downgrade, and "every version loads" rests on synthetic per-feature fixtures, not a committed corpus of real historical files. LightBurn leads at 8 on exactly the axis KerfDesk is weak — a mature format with a long track record of opening old files — and MillMage's cross-compatible `.mage` is a solid 7. The two pure senders, gSender (2, versioned settings backup only) and LaserGRBL (0, no project format at all), score low because within a competed sector a missing capability is a 0/2, not `N/R`; both are noted in the register as architectural absences, not defects.

Resource footprint (17.6) is the sector's — and arguably the audit's — sharpest single loss for KerfDesk, at 4. The memory *discipline* is real and code-verified (undo capped and structurally shared, transcripts capped at 200/500, a WeakMap display-decimation cache), but three facts pull it below every product except heavyweight XCS: an Electron/browser baseline that is inherently heavier than the native rivals, a **known unbounded leak** (the raster-preview canvas cache grows across every burn-setting change, a static ~800MB worst case) plus a ~704KB three.js chunk and a 5MB-quota autosave ceiling that costs photo-heavy projects their crash recovery, and — again — zero measurement. LaserGRBL is the featherweight leader at 9, the native pair (LightBurn/MillMage) sit at 7, lean-Electron gSender at 6, browser-tab Easel at 5. A photo-heavy user churning settings hits KerfDesk's cache leak as a real wall, which is exactly the "basic — real users hit walls" anchor.
